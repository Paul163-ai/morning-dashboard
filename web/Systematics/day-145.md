## Verse

"He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by
triumphing over them in him." — Colossians 2:15

## Explanation

Alongside substitution and ransom, Scripture describes the cross using
the imagery of conquest: Christ triumphing publicly over hostile
spiritual powers — the same rulers, authorities, and cosmic forces we
studied in April's look at spiritual warfare. What looked, on Good
Friday, like Christ's total defeat — arrested, mocked, executed — is
described here as the very moment of his decisive victory, disarming and
publicly shaming exactly the powers that seemed to be winning.

This picture (sometimes called Christus Victor) captures something the
substitution and ransom pictures don't emphasize as directly: the cross
as a cosmic battle, not merely a private transaction between God and an
individual sinner. Satan's accusations against us are answered, his
claim over those held captive to sin and death is broken, and his
apparent triumph at the cross becomes, in God's hands, his own undoing.
This is why the resurrection, which we'll study soon, matters so much —
it's the public vindication of a victory the cross itself had already
secured.

Held together with substitution and ransom rather than instead of them,
this picture reminds us the cross wasn't only about our individual
guilt being dealt with — it was a decisive victory in the larger
conflict between God and every power opposed to him.

## Prayer

Lord Jesus, at the cross you triumphed over every hostile power arrayed
against me. Let me live today in the confidence of a victory already
won. Amen.

## Reflection Question

Do you tend to think of the cross only in personal, individual terms, or
also as a cosmic victory over spiritual opposition? What difference
would holding both make?
