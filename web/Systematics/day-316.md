## Verse

"The last enemy to be destroyed is death." — 1 Corinthians 15:26

## Explanation

We turn now to the final major section of this year's study: the doctrine
of the future, everything Scripture tells us about how this age ends and
what comes after. We begin, fittingly, at the point every other doctrine
eventually has to reckon with — death itself. Scripture never asks
Christians to pretend death is natural, gentle, or simply part of life's
rhythm. Paul calls it an enemy, the last one standing in a long war,
because death was never part of God's original design; it entered through
sin (Romans 5:12) and remains, even for believers, something genuinely
hostile rather than a friend to be welcomed on its own terms.

And yet Paul places that enemy in a sentence about its destruction, not
its permanence. Christ's resurrection guarantees death's eventual defeat
as certainly as it guarantees his own victory over the grave, since Paul's
whole argument in this chapter is that Christ's rising is the first fruits
of a harvest still to come. This is the tension every Christian learns to
hold: death remains a real and painful enemy, grieved honestly, even as
we grieve as those who have certain hope (1 Thessalonians 4:13) that this
particular enemy's days are numbered.

## Prayer

Lord, death is still an enemy, and I don't have to pretend otherwise. But
thank you that its defeat is certain because your resurrection is
certain. Hold that hope before me. Amen.

## Reflection Question

Have you ever felt pressure — from yourself or others — to treat death as
something other than an enemy? How does calling it what Scripture calls
it change your grief?
