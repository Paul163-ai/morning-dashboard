## Verse

"If any speak in a tongue, let there be only two or three, and each in
turn, and let someone interpret. But if there is no one to interpret, let
each of them keep silent in church and speak to himself and to God." (1
Corinthians 14:27–28)

## Explanation

At Pentecost, tongues were known human languages, miraculously understood
by hearers who had never learned them (Acts 2:6–8), a sign, Paul later
says, particularly aimed at unbelieving Israel (1 Corinthians 14:22).
Elsewhere, Paul describes tongues used privately in prayer, edifying the
speaker even when "my mind is unfruitful" (1 Corinthians 14:14), but this
too is best read as the same category as Pentecost: a real, existing human
language, simply unknown to the speaker and to any listener, rather than a
distinct heavenly speech. When Paul writes of speaking "in the tongues of
men and of angels" (13:1), he is most naturally read as arguing
hypothetically: an extreme case chosen to make a point about love, the
way he elsewhere imagines wishing himself "accursed" for his kinsmen
(Romans 9:3), not describing a language he or the Corinthians actually
spoke.

Whatever the context, public or private, Paul's regulation here is
unambiguous: tongues spoken publicly without interpretation build up no
one but the speaker, and Paul would rather speak five intelligible words
that instruct others than ten thousand words no one present can
understand (14:19). The overriding value throughout this whole passage
isn't the gift's impressiveness but its usefulness, a principle that
applies to every gift, but is stated most sharply here: nothing in a
gathered service should be exercised in a way that leaves the church no
better equipped than before it happened.

## Prayer

Lord, whatever gifts are present in my church, let them build others up
rather than draw attention to the one exercising them. Amen.

## Reflection Question

Paul values intelligibility over impressiveness. Where else in your own
church life might that same principle need to be applied?
