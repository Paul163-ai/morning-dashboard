## Verse

"He was manifested in the flesh, vindicated by the Spirit, seen by
angels, proclaimed among the nations, believed on in the world, taken up
in glory." — 1 Timothy 3:16

## Explanation

This early Christian confession, likely a hymn quoted by Paul, moves
seamlessly between claims that make sense only of a divine person
("vindicated by the Spirit... seen by angels... taken up in glory") and
claims that make sense only of one who genuinely entered human history
("manifested in the flesh... proclaimed among the nations"). The
sentence structure itself reflects yesterday's doctrine: one subject, one
person, described across the entire range of divine and human
experience without any strain or contradiction.

This is worth pausing on because it shows the hypostatic union wasn't a
later theological invention imposed on simpler earlier accounts — it was
already implicit in how the earliest Christians spoke about Christ,
naturally moving between statements about his eternal glory and
statements about his historical, bodily, human life, treating both as
true of the very same "he."

Practically, this means we should resist the temptation to mentally
separate "Jesus the man" from "Christ the divine Son," relating to one
version more comfortably than the other. Scripture never allows that
separation. The one who wept at Lazarus's tomb and the one worshipped by
angels are the same single person, and our relationship with him is
always with that whole, undivided reality.

## Prayer

Lord Jesus, you are one person, seen in the flesh and glorified in
heaven. Help me relate to you as this single, undivided reality, not
splitting your humanity from your deity. Amen.

## Reflection Question

Do you tend to relate more naturally to Jesus' humanity or to his deity?
What would it look like to hold both together?
