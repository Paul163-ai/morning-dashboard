## Verse

"Let the whole assembly agree that this is a good plan."
(Acts 6:5, paraphrased context)

## Explanation

Over the next three days we'll survey the main historic patterns of
church government, beginning with congregationalism: the model in which
final decision-making authority rests with the whole gathered
congregation, typically exercised through voting on major matters:
appointing leaders, church discipline, significant decisions, rather
than being held exclusively by a smaller governing body. Acts 6 offers an
early example: when a practical dispute arose in the Jerusalem church,
the apostles brought the matter to the whole congregation to select
suitable men for the task, rather than deciding unilaterally themselves.

Proponents point to this pattern, and to passages describing the whole
church exercising discipline together (1 Corinthians 5:4–5, Matthew
18:17, where the matter goes finally "to the church"), as evidence that
Scripture envisions meaningful congregational involvement in significant
church decisions, guarding against concentrated, unaccountable authority
resting in too few hands.

Congregationalism is usually paired, in practice, with elder leadership
handling ordinary teaching and oversight, while the congregation retains
final authority over the most significant matters: a balance meant to
combine godly, qualified leadership with genuine accountability to the
whole body, rather than either extreme of pure democracy or unchecked
hierarchy.
