## Verse

"Then God said to him in the dream, 'Yes, I know that you have done this
in the integrity of your heart, and it was I who kept you from sinning
against me. Therefore I did not let you touch her.'" — Genesis 20:6

## Explanation

Yesterday's doctrine of total depravity could easily be
misread as claiming every person acts as wickedly as possible, all the
time. Genesis 20 shows why that's not what Scripture teaches. Abimelech,
a pagan king with no covenant relationship to God, is restrained by God
himself from committing a serious sin — not because Abimelech's own
character was untainted, but because God intervened to hold him back.
This is a clear biblical example of what theologians call common grace:
God's restraining, undeserved kindness extended to all people, believer
and unbeliever alike, holding back the full expression of the
corruption total depravity describes.

This explains something obvious from daily experience: unbelievers are
regularly capable of real kindness, honesty, and even moral courage,
sometimes putting believers to shame. Total depravity was never a claim
that people are utterly without any good impulse whatsoever; it's a
claim about the *extent* of sin's reach across every faculty, not the
*degree* to which that corruption is allowed to express itself. Common
grace is precisely why society doesn't collapse into the total chaos
that unrestrained depravity would otherwise produce.

This should make us grateful, rather than either naively optimistic
about human nature or bitterly cynical about it — the good we see in the
world, believer and unbeliever alike, is itself evidence of God's
ongoing restraining kindness.

## Prayer

Lord, thank you for restraining sin's full expression in this world
through your common grace. Give me neither naive optimism nor cynicism
about people, but honest gratitude for your kindness. Amen.

## Reflection Question

Where have you seen genuine goodness in someone who doesn't yet know
God — and can you recognise that as evidence of his restraining grace?
